How It Works

THE TIMING GATES

We design and build these ourselves, then donate them to programs that cannot afford timing equipment. This page explains what they measure and why it matters.

WHAT A TIMING GATE ACTUALLY DOES

A timing gate is a sensor on a stand. When an athlete passes through the beam, the gate registers the exact moment. Put two of them at a known distance apart and you have a split: the time it took to cover that distance.

That sounds simple, and the concept is. The difficulty is in the word "exact." A coach with a stopwatch reacts to what they see, and human reaction time runs somewhere around two tenths of a second on each end. Over a 40-yard dash, two tenths is the difference between a time that means something and a number you cannot compare to anything.

Our gates use LiDAR, which measures distance by timing a pulse of light. When the distance the sensor reads suddenly drops, something has passed in front of it. That reading happens hundreds of times a second, which is why the gate can tell you when an athlete crossed rather than approximately when.

WHY HAND TIMING IS NOT ENOUGH

Hand timing is not useless. For a coach tracking whether an athlete is roughly improving over a season, a stopwatch tells you something real. The problem shows up when you try to make decisions with the number.

Hand times run fast, typically by about two tenths of a second, because the timer starts late and stops early. Track and field has a standard conversion for this reason: hand times are adjusted upward when compared against fully automatic times. The adjustment exists because everyone agrees the two are not the same measurement.

The deeper issue is inconsistency. The same coach timing the same athlete twice will not produce the same error both times. So when an athlete runs a tenth faster than last week, you cannot tell whether they got faster or whether you were a tenth quicker on the button. Sprint improvements at the high school level are often measured in hundredths. A measurement tool with two tenths of noise cannot see them.

WHAT WE MEASURE

The gates run two modes. The first is a straight split between two gates: the clock starts at the first gate and stops at the second. This covers flying sprints at 10, 20, or 30 meters, the 40-yard dash, and any drill where you want a clean time over a set distance.

The second is a speed profile. The athlete starts on a button release, and the system records three numbers: start to the first gate, first gate to second gate, and start to the second gate. That separates acceleration from top-end speed. Two athletes can run the same 40 and get there completely differently, one exploding out and fading, the other building late. A single number hides that. Three numbers show it.

The timing logic lives in software rather than in the hardware, so new modes can be added without new equipment.

HOW WE KNOW IT IS ACCURATE

A specification on a page is a claim, not evidence. Before we were willing to hand these to a coach, we wanted a number we could defend.

Gravity provided one. An object dropped from a known height falls on a schedule physics predicts precisely, so we dropped objects through the gates and compared what the system recorded against what the equations said should have happened. The gates came in accurate to roughly five milliseconds.

For context: five milliseconds is about a quarter of the length of a single video frame at standard frame rates. Hand timing is off by more than two hundred milliseconds. That gap, between five and two hundred, is the reason this project exists.

WHY THIS EQUIPMENT IS NORMALLY EXPENSIVE

Commercial timing systems run from about $350 to well over $1,500 per unit, and many add a monthly subscription for the software that reads them. For a well funded program that is a line item. For a public high school splitting a small budget across every sport, it is out of reach entirely.

The cost is not in the physics. The sensor, the microcontroller, and the radio that lets two gates talk to each other are inexpensive parts. The cost is in the markup chain: the company that designs it, the company that manufactures it, the distributor, the reseller, and the brand.

We do the design, the firmware, the circuit boards, and the enclosures ourselves. Nobody at EqualSplit takes a salary. That is the entire explanation for the price difference, and it is why we can give sets away.

HOW TO GET A SET

If you coach a program that cannot afford timing equipment, apply for a free unit. We read every application, and there is no cost to you, including shipping.

Programs with the budget to pay can buy a set through our separate store site. The amount above what the parts cost goes into the donation program, so a program that pays is funding a program that cannot.

Either way, the setup guide and safety information are freely available, and you can reach us at contact@equalsplit.org with questions.
